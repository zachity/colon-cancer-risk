\begin{abstract}
This project investigates how modifiable behavioral factors, specifically obesity and smoking, relate to age adjusted colorectal cancer incidence across U.S. states. Using BRFSS 2022 behavioral health survey data \parencite{cdc2023brfss} and USCS cancer incidence data retrieved through CDC WONDER \parencite{cdc2024uscs}, the project constructs a curated, integrated dataset suitable for epidemiological exploration. The work reflects the full lifecycle of data curation including acquisition, cleaning, integration, metadata generation, workflow automation, and ethical evaluation \parencite{lord2004}. The resulting curated dataset is documented, reproducible, and packaged using metadata standards such as DataCite \parencite{group2021} and the FAIR principles \parencite{wilkinson2016}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Project Motivation}

This semester, I experienced a profound personal loss when my mother passed away after battling cancer. Walking alongside her through diagnosis, treatment, and the progression of the disease made the realities of cancer far more immediate and deeply human than statistics alone can convey. Her experience motivated me to use this project as an opportunity to engage more deeply with cancer related public health data, not only as an academic exercise but also as a way to better understand cancer risk and prevention in the United States.

Colorectal cancer is among the most prevalent and most preventable forms of cancer nationwide. Although this project does not attempt to model individual risk or contribute clinical insights, it explores a question that emerged naturally from personal reflection and public health interest:

\begin{quote}
How do modifiable behavioral factors, such as obesity and smoking, relate to variation in colorectal cancer incidence across U.S. states?
\end{quote}

To investigate this question, two national datasets were curated and integrated: the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System \parencite{cdc2023brfss} and the U.S. Cancer Statistics registry accessed through CDC WONDER \parencite{cdc2024uscs}. Both sources are widely used in public health research but present substantial heterogeneity, documentation challenges, and structural complexity, making them well suited for a data curation focused project.

\section{Dataset Profiles}

\subsection{BRFSS 2022 Behavioral Health Indicators}

BRFSS \parencite{cdc2023brfss} is the largest continuously collected public health survey system in the world. Raw BRFSS files are released in SAS Transport (XPT) format and require specialized extraction and interpretation.

Variables used include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item STATE: FIPS coded state identifier
    \item RFSMOK3: recoded smoking status
    \item OBESITY3: obesity indicator based on BMI
\end{itemize}

\subsection{USCS and CDC WONDER Incidence Data}

Age adjusted colorectal cancer incidence data were obtained from USCS via CDC WONDER \parencite{cdc2024uscs}. These data provide standardized annual incidence rates per 100,000 population.

\section{Data Curation Workflow}

\subsection{Extraction}

The script \texttt{brfss\_2022\_state\_summary.py} extracts BRFSS indicators and computes state level summaries \parencite{cdc2023brfss}.  
The script \texttt{cdc\_colon\_state\_summaries.py} processes USCS incidence exports and handles suppressed values \parencite{cdc2024uscs}.

\subsection{Integration}

The script \texttt{combine\_state\_summaries.py} merges BRFSS and USCS summaries, resolving inconsistent identifiers and formatting differences. This reflects the data wrangling challenges discussed in Terrizzano et al. \parencite{terrizzano2015}.

\section{Lifecycle Model Alignment (M1)}

The project follows the CRISP DM framework \parencite{chapman2000}, emphasizing iterative refinement in data understanding and preparation.  
Plale and Kouper \parencite{plale2017} highlight the role of lifecycle thinking in managing multistage data processes, which shaped decisions throughout acquisition, documentation, and dissemination.  
Hanson et al. \parencite{hanson2012} demonstrate how poor documentation undermines reuse, reinforcing the importance of metadata and structured directories in this project.

\section{Ethical, Legal, and Policy Considerations (M2)}

Ethical considerations included avoiding ecological fallacy, acknowledging biases in self reported BRFSS data, and respecting CDC constraints around suppressed values.  
The Menlo Report \parencite{bailey2012} guided ethical framing, emphasizing respect for persons, beneficence, justice, and responsibility to public interest.

\section{Data Models and Abstractions (M3 to M5)}

The curated dataset uses a relational tabular model in which each state is an entity with behavioral and incidence attributes. This design aligns with identifier and metadata best practices from DataCite \parencite{group2021} and Schema.org \parencite{schemaorg2024}.

\section{Metadata and Documentation (M8)}

Metadata were developed using the DataCite schema \parencite{group2021} and FAIR principles \parencite{wilkinson2016}. Documentation includes a README, a codebook, and clearly defined script inputs and outputs.

\section{Findings, Challenges, and Lessons Learned}

Initial exploratory analyses showed positive associations between obesity, smoking, and cancer incidence. Figures \ref{fig:obesity} and \ref{fig:smoking} display these relationships.

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.}  
States with higher obesity prevalence tend to show higher incidence, though substantial variation suggests moderating influences such as screening rates or socioeconomic differences.

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.}  
Smoking also shows a positive association with colorectal cancer incidence, though the slope is weaker than for obesity and outliers suggest additional underlying factors.

\subsection*{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item State level ecological data cannot be used to infer individual risk.
    \item BRFSS data are self reported and subject to recall and social desirability bias.
    \item CDC WONDER suppresses small counts, reducing granularity for small states.
    \item Only two behavioral factors were analyzed; many confounders remain unmodeled.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility, Automation, and Dissemination (M12 and M15)}

The full workflow is automated through structured scripts and reproducible directories. The environment can be recreated using:

\begin{verbatim}
pip install -r environment/requirements.txt
\end{verbatim}

The curated dataset is fully regenerable from raw inputs following the steps described in the README.

\section{Conclusion}

This project demonstrates the full lifecycle of data curation including acquisition, wrangling, integration, documentation, ethics, and dissemination. Grounded in course readings \parencite{bailey2012, hanson2012, plale2017, terrizzano2015}, it highlights the importance of transparent, well documented, and ethically responsible data work.

\section{Raw Data Retrieval Instructions}

BRFSS 2022 must be downloaded manually from:  
\url{https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/annual_2022.html}  
Save as \texttt{data/raw/LLCP2022.XPT}.

USCS incidence data must be exported from:  
\url{https://wonder.cdc.gov/cancer.html}  
Save as \texttt{data/raw/uscs\_1999\_2022\_full.csv}.
